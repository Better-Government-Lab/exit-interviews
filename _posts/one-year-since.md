#### Chapter 1

# Wins in a decade of civic tech

Participants shared many experiences of personal and professional successes. The work they did was sometimes profoundly meaningful, confirming for them what it means to be an American, or be in service to others. They found professional success in their ways of working, team culture, and while  many positions or projects were dissolved, they saw their work making tangible and measurable impact across the problems they sought to solve.

## Pro-social motivations

> “I was always very oriented around how we can use technology to advance democracy and improve people’s access to information” (5).

The civic tech experience showed that the government can attract highly skilled tech workers by leveraging appeals to prosocial motivation. Nearly every person we interviewed expressed prosocial motivation for entering government roles. Interviewees frequently commented that they and other civil servants stayed in government roles because of passion for the work, at times forgoing higher salaries in the private sector to do so.

## Practices

> “Before me, because I was the only designer in the division, there were no process maps, and so there were all of these gaps...our employees were making workarounds and hacking things together, but mapping it out, I was able to show leadership, look, this is what's happening. This is how people are fixing it. This is how we can make their lives easier and increase the throughput.” (P09)

In many ways, civic technologists popularized design thinking in public administration, demonstrating that technical knowledge from the private sector could serve as a knowledge base for e-government projects (Meijer, 2025). The civic tech movement also sought to drive digital transformation through collaborative approaches to modernization projects. This included shifting from sequential (“waterfall”) methods towards agile innovation management (Mergel, 2016; Mergel et al., 2021). It also involved differently engaging citizens as stakeholders in government projects. Arguments that e-governance may activate citizens as co-producers of public value (e.g., Pestoff et al., 2012; Meijer, 2015) predate the civic tech movement’s entry into the U.S. federal government. However, the movement’s emphasis on inclusion-oriented principles—such as human-centered design—represented new approaches to integrating user experience in digital transformation efforts.

While workers shared with us plenty of examples of transitioning services from analog or manual processes to digital workflows, they also spoke about the practices, culture, and norms they brought that were not widespread within federal agencies. Human centered design, product management, testing, iterating, shipping, and measuring were core elements of the digital practices interviewees said contributed to their success

## Culture

> “I think the culture we developed will outlast all of the work we did.”

It was often the culture of digital service teams set up within the White House (USDS), TTS (18F), CMS (DSAC), and the Defense department (DDS) that supported and fostered these practices and especially from those with experience at 18F, in a respectful and collaborative (remote-first) workplace with colleagues with impressive expertise from the private and public sector alike.

#### Chapter 2

# Why is it so hard to get tech done in government?

Federal bureaucracies are not known for speed. Whether you’re a citizen who has navigated Kafkaesque systems to get benefits or a public servant dealing with procurement rules, you have likely experienced slow, rigid government processes. In contrast, private sector technology is often characterized by rapid, sometimes risky innovation—or as Mark Zuckerberg puts it, a drive to “move fast and break things.” Many civic technologists faced a learning curve applying modern tech principles in highly regulated agency environments with disconnected data systems. While some of the frictions they encountered are classic management challenges in public administration, other barriers are unique to technology projects.

## Technologists meet government red tape

Hiring has long been a challenge across the federal government. Technologists from the private sector often accepted lower pay and slow hiring processes in exchange for a chance to serve the public. Still, many were frustrated by bureaucratic barriers to hiring and keeping the talent needed to build strong internal technology teams. Digital service workers hired through programs like the Presidential Innovation Fellows or through agencies like 18F or the U.S. Digital Service were usually limited to short-term roles. Political appointees at agencies such as the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services typically served only two to four years, making it hard to complete long-term projects because staff felt their “clocks were ticking.” Strict education requirements, veterans’ preference rules, and hiring freezes further slowed hiring and prevented some data scientists from entering government service.

For both hiring contractors and purchasing technology, government procurement is intensely bureaucratic. Digital service workers often felt constrained by short project timelines, small teams, and narrowly defined work. While the civic tech movement introduced agency leaders to ideas like agile development, large contracts with lengthy approval processes, detailed requirements, and proprietary systems made it difficult to apply agile methods in practice. Writing clearer requests for proposals helped, but it was not enough. Without outcome-oriented models for product development, technology and data were often siloed within individual agencies or departments. Strict data security rules further limited teams’ ability to share information and reduce administrative burden for staff and the public.

Finally, government agencies themselves are complex organizations. Digital service teams are usually small units within much larger agencies and must follow decisions made by senior leaders and oversight bodies such as the Office of Management and Budget. Unlike private companies conducting user experience research, government teams must follow the Paperwork Reduction Act, which limits when and how agencies can collect information from the public. ## Culture issues

Many of the tech workers we interviewed spoke positively about the welcoming and innovative cultures in their offices. However, we also heard that some senior leaders resisted the civic tech approach. Several interviewees felt their agencies relied on top-down, hierarchical decision-making, which limited the feedback loops needed for agile development. In contrast, offices with consensus-based cultures sometimes did not give digital service teams enough authority to make decisions about technology products, treating the work as short-term projects instead. Others described a sense of defeatism among agencies that had experienced failed technology acquisitions or poor system implementations. While it is common for large organizations like government agencies to embrace change slowly and avoid risk, these tendencies can stand in the way of civic tech innovation.

## Today's problems, yesterday's technologies

Too many government agencies still depend on outdated technology systems. The technologists we interviewed identified several reasons for this, including rigid procurement rules, failed modernization efforts, data silos, limited funding, and changes in legal requirements over time. They described legacy systems as being “bootstrapped together,” “duct taped,” and heavily dependent on manual processes. Some interviewees also said that agency leaders were reluctant to use their discretion to ease the path to modernization, such as by allowing more frequent software updates or technology releases. Using outdated tech to run modern programs does more than create frustration for staff, contractors, and the public. Legacy systems often require specialized skills to maintain, making them costly and time-consuming. In addition, the lack of shared technology and data systems across agencies makes it harder for digital service teams to align standards and share insights, which can ultimately weaken program outcomes.
