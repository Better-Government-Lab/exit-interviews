
<p class="subhed">Voices from the frontlines of federal digital service work including the successes, challenges, and lessons learned from workers who separated from their roles between February–September 2025.</p>

## Introduction

For more than a decade, the federal government was part of the growing civic tech movement: teams of designers, engineers, researchers, product managers, and long-time public servants focused on improving technologies and services across federal agencies.
The 2025 presidential transition abruptly dismantled many of the federal teams and programs at the center of this movement.  In response, the Better Government Lab and the Beeck Center for Social Impact + Innovation launched the Federal Civic Tech Exit Project. Over six months, we interviewed 45 former digital service workers to learn about the impact they made, the successes and barriers they encountered, the practices they helped develop, the circumstances of their departures, and the work left unfinished.
In addition, we also focused in-depth on one major civic tech project, the creation of a free digital tax reporting service called Direct File at the IRS. In addition to those interviewed for the Civic Tech Exit project, we interviewed 20 additional civic technologists and policymakers to understand the creation and ultimate elimination of Direct File.

Here we share some of the major themes emerging from our interviews, and invite you to explore the voices and perspectives of federal tech workers who, during their time in government, worked diligently towards improving digital services for the American public.

We view this work as part of a broader effort to better understand the relationship between technology and government. The archive of interviews is a public good available to all who care about this topic, including media, policymakers, civic technologists and researchers. The Better Government Lab is undertaking its own analysis of the interviews (you can find links to our papers below).

#### Chapter 1

<h1 id="wins">Wins</h1>

The successes of the civic tech movement extended beyond the creation or improvement of digital tools. When asked about their greatest successes and the most satisfying aspects of their work, interviewees reflected on the changes they saw as outcomes of their work: a growing culture of transparency and collaboration, capacity building inside agencies, the spread of agile and human centered design practices, and reduced burdens for government workers and the public.

## Growing culture

> “The culture we developed will outlast all of the work we did."

Technologists linked many of their successes to a culture of collaboration and openness that challenged the hierarchical nature of government bureaucracy. Teams often "worked in the open," collaborated across disciplines, and communicated frequently. Those working within 18F in particular, described their workplace as respectful and inspiring, where practitioners at the top of their field did meaningful work. Often, teams shared their work broadly through open-source code, playbooks, procurement guides, UX methods, Slack channels, and communities of practice that were shared across not just the federal government, but state governments and the private sector.
While groups like 18F and USDS helped popularize these approaches, civic technologists and customer experience professionals across the federal government adapted and sustained them. What spread was more than PDFs and github repos, it was a set of practical tools, a shared language, and a basic alignment among hundreds of federal technologists on how government could work better to deliver services to the public

## Collaboration

> “We're here to build skills with the agencies we're working with."

Despite their valuable tech skills, teams could only be successful by relying on knowledge and buy-in from government workers to successfully navigate complex government problems. Notably, this was a lesson that federal civic tech workers learned over time, a departure from the “savior complex” that many said marked the early days of 18F and USDS.  Interviewees shared how their work centered on the ideas of caseworkers, analysts, policy makers, and program leaders with deep knowledge of longstanding problems. When digital service workers could bring new approaches, UX and technical expertise, and often executive level support, they could move these ideas forward. Building alongside civil servants resulted in solutions that were more sustainable, and agencies that were better equipped to carry the work forward.

<p class="voices">
  <p class="voices__title">Voices of civic tech</p>

  <p class="voices__body--1">“Very few of the ideas were our ideas. They were a caseworker's idea. They were the policy director's idea. They were, you know, some analyst’s idea that everyone had, but they never had the space or the focus or the structure to be able to raise that idea, have it actually validated through implementation.” - Interviewee 05</p>
</p>

## New practices

> “Being ready to abandon what you are doing if it isn't working is a central tenet of agile”

Civic technologists described bringing practices into government that were not yet widespread across federal agencies. Human-centered design, agile development, product management, testing, and iteration were among the core practices they said contributed to their success. Interviewees also described these practices as particularly well suited to the public sector environment and its constraints. With a goal of deliberate and incremental improvement, technologists focused on understanding the problem, starting small, scoping projects, testing, and iterating. User testing, in particular, provided an alternative to traditional methods of public engagement by directly involving  the public and frontline staff in process improvement and product development.

## Reducing burden

> "Every project I was on was trying to reduce [administrative burden], whether we said it or not."

Reducing administrative burden was one of the clearest ways civic technologists understood, communicated about, and measured the success of their work. Depending on the project, they described reducing burdens for members of the public, non governmental workers, or federal or state civil servants. This included described simplifying forms, shortening processing times, reducing demand on call centers, and improving reporting. One interviewee recounted how improvements to grant recipient data reporting saved time, improved accuracy, and enhanced agency data insights. We also heard about efforts to reduce burden through shared federal services and simplified contracts that would reduce common low value tasks across government like sending text messages or uploading and processing documents. In these and many more ways, interviewees were seeking reductions in administrative burden to show progress and success.

  <p class="voices__body--1">"When we're talking about customer experience, and employee experience...the best way we found to talk about it was the burdens."</p>

#### Chapter 2

<h1 id="barriers">Barriers to progress</h1>

Civic technologists described a learning curve applying modern tech principles in highly regulated agency environments with disconnected data systems. Friction in workplace culture, outdated technology, as well as hiring rules, were often cited as barriers to larger federal technology transformation.

## Risk and resistance

> “It was interagency infighting, or risk aversion, bureaucratic inertia, anything that you can expect in government.”

Many of the tech workers we interviewed described the welcoming and innovative cultures in their offices. However, we also heard many examples of resistance to the civic tech approach caused by differences in organization culture and an aversion to risk.
Government bureaucracy often clashed with civic tech work because authority over digital service projects was distributed across a dizzying amount of government groups. Interviewees recalled that it was often unclear who held final authority to advance change. Technologists shared multiple examples of how hierarchical decision-making limited the feedback loops needed for agile development. On the other hand, slow consensus-based cultures sometimes did not give digital service teams enough authority to make decisions.
Risk aversion was another common barrier interviewees described, produced by a history of failed technology projects, the threat of public backlash, a reward structure to maintain the status quo, and established mechanisms for outsourcing risk through contracts. Interviewees spoke of a valedictorian culture and shared defeatism as chief problems with one interviewee saying most mid-level managers they worked with would “rather have a guarantee of failure than a chance of success.”

<p class="voices">
  <p class="voices__title">Voices of civic tech</p>

  <p class="voices__body--1"> “Was it Operations who got to make the call? Was it the CIO’s office? Was it the Chief Transformation Office? It was often difficult to get buy-in for our work, because it seemed like there was an endless list of people who could say no or throw up blockers and no clear person who could say yes”</p>
</p>

## Buying tech

> “The federal contracting apparatus was by far, individually, the most frustrating part of the job”

Interviewees identified procurement, and the government’s long reliance on contractors to build and maintain technology, as one of the biggest barriers in their work. They did not believe that better requests for proposals (RFP) or different vendors would be enough. Without technical and product expertise in-house, agencies struggled to evaluate vendors, direct delivery, or hold vendors accountable when work fell short. Interviewees felt that policy makers and program managers cannot continue to outsource all technology expertise to vendors and needed to fundamentally shift from managing vendor contracts to managing digital products themselves as “product owners” and even  leading delivery in-house.

The technologists we interviewed shared many examples of how outdated technology and systems caused significant burden and expenses when tasked with supporting modern government programs and services.  The systems themselves were described  as being “duct taped together” and heavily dependent on manual processes and specialized skills, making them costly and time-consuming to maintain. In addition, they described examples where the lack of shared technology, data, or standards across agencies blocked transformation efforts, buried data in silos, and weakened program outcomes. Ultimately, workers provided many examples of how outdated technology systems cost the public one way or another via wasted efficiency, tax dollars, or by enabling predatory practices in industry.

<p class="voices">
  <p class="voices__title">Voices of civic tech</p>

  <p class="voices__body--1"> “there are… these cottage industry companies that are essentially preying on governmental failure, like, hey, the government has a really terrible interface for this. We're going to sell you a nicer interface that is going to work better. We're going to foist all the hard parts still onto the government, but we're going to make it easy for you, but we're going to charge you.  People will pay for that every time.”</p>
</p>

## Short term something

> “We had to fight tooth and nail to introduce new job titles to them”

Hiring was identified as another major barrier to digital service work. Many product managers, designers, researchers, and engineers we spoke with accepted lower pay and term-limited roles in exchange for a chance to serve the public. While most term limited workers hoped to stay in government, permanent positions matching their skills were extremely rare. When agencies were able to open new roles, they often created only one or two new positions that were insufficient to manage large-scale digital transformation work in an otherwise unchanged bureaucracy.

With their “clocks ticking,” term limited teams often pursued high visibility programs, short projects, and ‘quick wins.’ These interventions could deliver meaningful results, but sometimes left the policies, systems, and operating structures beneath a service untouched. This led to a frustrating cycle for civic tech workers and longtime civil servants: the constant “rediscovery of something that’s already known to be a problem.”  While USDS particularly was able to achieve positive outcomes through leveraging their executive office power: marshalling resources and stakeholders to work on critical issues, this was too often seen as short term. Some questioned whether some short term projects ultimately caused harm. Others described pre-existing distrust from career civil servants, resistance to new ways of working, and a sense that the short term tours and newly hired digital service workers were leaving the civil service behind. Ultimately, the term limited and new permanent roles were the most vulnerable in the Trump administration’s mass layoffs because those roles often fell within a probationary status.

<p class="voices">
  <p class="voices__title">Voices of civic tech</p>

  <p class="voices__body--1">I think a lot of the work of digital quote unquote to date has been kind of superficial, because I think it's been focusing on, hey, how can I build a website without stepping on the toes of people who might have written those past policies, might have written those past statutes”</p>

#### Chapter 3

<h1 id="end">The end of an era</h1>

## Chaos

> “Departure from government was one of the worst experiences of my life.”

Many technologists described the first few months of 2025 as chaotic and confusing with conflicting messaging and lack of direction from agency leadership. Term limited or newly created roles, which digital service workers often held, were lamented as the mechanism through which they were easily removed. Most were deeply disappointed at having to leave work that felt meaningful. Many described their departures as traumatic, distressing, or coerced. Widespread confusion about the deferred resignation offer compounded the chaos with many interviewees leaving after concluding they had no other reasonable option. Both longtime federal workers and newer employees transitioning from the private sector characterized the presidential transition as uniquely cruel and chaotic. Although digital service workers focused on modernization and efficiency, interviewees saw little interest in assessing their skills, prior work, or potential value.

<p class="voices">
  <p class="voices__title">Voices of civic tech</p>

  <p class="voices__body--1">“It was chaos. We were constantly fighting fires, and I thought it was going to settle down to a point where, okay, is the dust going to settle to get anything done? And after a couple months, I just realized it wasn't going to. We weren't waiting for the dust to settle. We were just standing in dust.” </p>
</p>

## DOGE

> “This was more like being bought by a company that was a rival…[It] was the cruelty that bothered me more than anything else.” (P14)

Most of the civic tech workers we spoke with approached DOGE with an open mind. They initially hoped that DOGE would add capacity and further digital government and efficiency efforts already underway. Many technologists we spoke with, especially those with longer tenure, felt that radical change was necessary and that the efforts of the civic tech movement hadn’t been successful enough.
Once DOGE started operating, civic tech views became more negative.  Several felt DOGE interests lay narrowly in using AI on government data, putting the solution before the problem, and a lack of interest in real problems. Several shared they were worried about the personal data of Americans being used in ways the public nor congress agreed to. Some participants recounted feeling disturbed by the actions of the new administration or allies, especially those who had to take down or remove data that didn’t reflect the new administration's priorities. Though efficiency was a stated goal of DOGE, participants felt that because so many efficiency and cost-saving digital projects and teams were dismissed, the real goal was access to government databases.
Most participants felt strongly that the new efforts placed little value on delivering services, understanding complexity, or the experiences of the American public. In fact, technologists we spoke with shared that the approaches were reminiscent of the negative tendencies of early civic tech efforts, where technology was seen as the answer, without interest or understanding of the deeper issues at play

<p class="voices">
  <p class="voices__title">Voices of civic tech</p>

  <p class="voices__body--1">I don't really know DOGE's approach to digital services delivery… As far as I know, they're more in the un-delivery business of taking things out than putting things in. …Is it weird to cut almost all your CX people from all of government and then create a CX initiative? Sure.”</p>
</p>

## Not done

> “I absolutely would work in federal government again. I’m not done.”

Despite the negative experiences of leaving their roles, many wanted to return to federal service, and hoped that lessons from the past decade of civic tech work would inform future digital service efforts. They saw tech as a means to help the public in ways consistent with their personal motivations. Most were not drawn to government itself, but with the opportunities government provided to solve problems, and their sense that their tech skills could help with a traditional public sector weakness.
What would empowered technology teams look like in practice? When asked about their hopes for the future of civic tech, they highlighted a need to shift toward longer time horizons for technology projects. Moving from pilots and project-based work to system-wide modernization, as well as developing permanent career pathways for tech specialist roles that could, among other things, manage vendor relationships better.
The result would be integrating civic tech practices into the core of government operations, rather than ad hoc engagements and temporary assignments. The interviewees also emphasized that user experience research, equitable service delivery, and the reduction in administrative burden should continue to steer digital service work in the future. Modernizing procurement, data sharing, and compliance frameworks would eliminate some bureaucratic and delivery challenges while supporting a continued focus on human-centered design.

<p class="voices">
  <p class="voices__title">Voices of civic tech</p>

  <p class="voices__body--1">“I don't have any good answers. I think personally we can't go back to the way things were. I'm not saying we can't organize, it just can't be the same.”</p>
</p>

#### Appendix

<h1 id="project">About the project</h1>

## Who we interviewed

Interview participants held roles in agencies across the federal government and identified their work as digital service or civic technology. They typically came from outside of government, and often held relatively short term fellowships. Many had worked at 18F and the U.S. Digital Service, two groups often seen as the vanguard of the federal civic tech movement. For a sense of scale, 18F employed fewer than 100 people when it was disbanded, while USDS had more than 200 at the start of the second Trump administration. We also interviewed practitioners working in digital service roles at established agencies such as the Internal Revenue Service and Social Security Administration, including “teams of one” hired as an agency’s sole designer, product manager, or other digital specialist. Participants also varied in their federal tenure, allowing us to hear from both longtime civil servants and people who had entered government more recently.
